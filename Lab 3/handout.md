# Mixed Aldol Condensation Reaction

# Purpose

To investigate the aldol condensation reaction as a method of forming new carbon-carbon bonds. 

# Background

Most organic compounds found in nature are derived from simple organic starting materials such as $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ or building blocks like sugars and amino acids. The creation of more complex molecules requires the formation of new carbon-carbon bonds. When these same compounds are made in a laboratory, different starting materials are used but creating new carbon-carbon bonds is still an important component of organic synthesis. The aldol condensation is one of the more useful synthetic methods for generating new carbon-carbon bonds. 

A condensation reaction occurs when two molecules are joined together and a molecule of water is produced. The aldol condensation starts with two molecules containing carbonyl groups $(\mathrm{C} = \mathrm{O})$ and results in the formation of a carbon-carbon double bond and the loss of a molecule of water. The general mechanism and equation are shown in Figure 28. 


Figure 28.


![image](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-02-21/3cc57f32-69df-4c5d-bb9f-355d293a9a85/105be122a36ecf477a3e79b16f17ebcda45e29637b087ba240ba5fb88f15ece8.jpg)


In this laboratory procedure, acetone undergoes a double aldol condensation with two molecules of benzaldehyde to form dibenzalacetone. The reaction is catalyzed by sodium hydroxide. The first step in this aldol condensation is the generation of the acetone enolate ion by removing the acidic proton from the methyl group $\alpha$ to the carbonyl group in acetone (the $\alpha$ position is next to the carbonyl group). This proton is acidic because aldehydes and ketones exist in solution as an equilibrium mixture of two isomeric forms, the keto form and the enol form (Figure 29). Even though the 

percentage of the enol form at equilibrium is quite small, it does lead to some acidic nature in the proton. Under alkaline (basic) conditions, a hydrogen which is $\alpha$ to a carbonyl carbon, can be removed by a strong base. 


Figure 29.


![image](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-02-21/3cc57f32-69df-4c5d-bb9f-355d293a9a85/dea8d56ee59b60d31f7413cf607017321e8e45db30b95ca67ee2d2432526dc20.jpg)


The second step of this aldol condensation is the nucleophilic addition of the acetone enolate ion to benzaldehyde (Figure 30). This step generates the new carbon-carbon bond. The driving force for this step is that the enolate ion is a good nucleophile (positive-area seeker) and it attacks the carbon atom of a carbonyl group which has a partial positive charge due to bond polarization. The carbon-oxygen bond is polarized in the direction of $C^+ - O^-$ because oxygen is more electronegative than carbon and attracts the bonding electrons more strongly. 


Figure 30.


![image](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-02-21/3cc57f32-69df-4c5d-bb9f-355d293a9a85/3bd1ce186f32f3453685474839af51850d096440abf7840b29a2025bad4a48ec.jpg)


The next step of the aldol reaction is a two-part condensation step which generates a carbon-carbon double bond and produces a water molecule (Figure 31). The anion from the previous step is protonated to produce a $\beta$ -hydroxycarbonyl product (the $\beta$ -position is two carbons from the carbonyl group). Under basic conditions, the hydroxyl group is a good leaving group because of the acidity of the proton next to the hydroxy group and the stability of the resulting $\alpha, \beta$ -unsaturated ketone. 


Figure 31.


![image](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-02-21/3cc57f32-69df-4c5d-bb9f-355d293a9a85/6a92cdb14eb03864f9e11fb80c749c749785c1045d74beffecb43bcfc875c29d.jpg)


The other side of this molecule also contains an enolizable methyl group and will also undergo an aldol condensation reaction with another benzaldehyde molecule to form the final product, dibenzalacetone (Figure 32). 


Figure 32.


![image](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-02-21/3cc57f32-69df-4c5d-bb9f-355d293a9a85/668c5e907e9b32dfd53ef26f171fe6061c4748f9edfc10b52ff7fae71826dedc.jpg)


One problem with the aldol reaction is that all carbonyl groups that contain an $\alpha$ -hydrogen will undergo a condensation reaction with any other carbonyl group or with itself. For example, if two different carbonyl compounds (e.g., A and B) are used in an aldol condensation reaction, as many as four different products can be formed if both starting compounds can form enolates (e.g., AA, AB, BA, BB). In order for the aldol reaction between two different carbonyl compounds to be useful, usually one of the starting carbonyl compounds must have no $\alpha$ -hydrogen atoms and therefore cannot form the starting enolate. In such cases, there are only two possible products, the desired mixed condensation (AB) and the self-condensation (AA) product. 

In this laboratory procedure, there are no $\alpha$ -hydrogens on the benzaldehyde molecule; therefore only the acetone forms an enolate. The resulting nucleophilic acetone enolate ion will attack an available carbonyl group. However, with two molecules containing carbonyl groups available, benzaldehyde and acetone, care must be taken to "encourage" the acetone enolate to attack the benzaldehyde carbonyl. This is achieved two ways. First, an excess of sodium hydroxide is used to quickly convert most of the acetone into the enolate which no longer attracts nucleophiles. Second, a slight excess of benzaldehyde is used to make sure that the quantity of benzaldehyde always greatly exceeds the quantity of acetone available. 

Until the final step which produces the double bond, all the steps of the aldol reaction are in equilibrium and the entire reaction is reversible. The driving force for the reaction is the stability of the final $\alpha, \beta$ -unsaturated ketone. 

After an acetone enolate ion attacks the benzaldehyde carbonyl group, the sodium hydroxide catalyzes the elimination of a hydroxide group and the formation of the carbon-carbon double bond. This whole process is repeated when another sodium hydroxide molecule attacks the other $\alpha$ -hydrogen on the other side of the acetone carbonyl. Again, the resulting ion can attack another benzaldehyde molecule to form the final product, dibenzalacetone.

# Safety and Chemical Hazards

Acetone is flammable and a dangerous fire risk, toxic by ingestion and inhalation. Benzaldehyde is flammable and is toxic by inhalation, ingestion or skin absorption. Sodium hydroxide is a corrosive solid, skin burns are possible; much heat evolves when added to water or alcohol, very dangerous to eyes. Wear eye and skin protection when using this substance. Always place the immersion heater in the water first before plugging it in. Always wear chemical splash goggles, chemical-resistant gloves and a chemical-resistant apron. 

# Experimental Procedure

# Procedure - Setup

1. Add approximately $300~\mathrm{mL}$ of deionized water to a $400\mathrm{-mL}$ beaker. Place an immersion heater in the water, plug it in and allow the water to come to a boil. Do not plug in the immersion heater until after it is placed in the water. 

2. Place $2.0 \, \text{g}$ (2 mL) of benzaldehyde and $0.5 \, \text{g}$ (0.6 mL) of acetone in a small reaction vial. If performing yield calculations, weigh both reactants as they are being added to the vial. Use glass Pasteur pipets, not plastic pipets, to add the organic reagent to the vial. 

3. Add $0.75\mathrm{mL}$ of sodium hydroxide-ethanol solution to the reaction vial. 

4. Seal the vial with a Teflon-coated cap. Make sure the cap is on tight. Shake the reaction mixture. Attach a copper wire to the vial to serve as a handle. 

5. Use the copper wire handle to place the sealed vial in the beaker containing boiling water and immersion heater. If at any time during the reaction a small stream of bubbles begins to flow out from around the reaction vial cap, remove the vial from the boiling water, allow it to cool and tighten the cap. 

6. Remove the reaction vial from the boiling water after about 10 minutes. Invert the reaction vial several times to mix, tighten the cap and then place the reaction vial back into the boiling water. 

7. After another 10 minutes, remove the vial from the hot water and place it on the table top. Allow the vial to cool for a few minutes. 

8. Place the vial in a beaker of cold water for three minutes. Remove the vial from the cold water and carefully remove the cap. 

9. Prepare $10~\mathrm{mL}$ ice water in a small test tube. 

10. Pour the reaction liquid from the vial into the test tube containing the ice water. If the product in the vial is already solid, remove with a small spatula and place in the ice water. 

11. Rinse the reaction vial with water to remove all of the product. 

# Isolation of Product

12. Shake the test tube containing ice water and product vigorously. A yellow precipitate will form. If a yellow solid does not form, place the test tube in an ice water bath for several minutes or until a solid forms. 

13. Allow the precipitate to settle. Decant the liquid using a micro-tip pipet and wash the yellow solid with another $10\mathrm{mL}$ of cold water. 

14. Allow the product to settle. Decant the liquid one last time and place the product onto a watch glass or doubled piece of filter paper. 

15. Allow the product to dry. Drying can be hastened by placing the product under an incandescent lamp. 

# Purification and Analysis

16. The yellow product is dibenzalacetone. Weigh the crude product, calculate crude yield. 

17. Crude dibenzalacetone may be purified by recrystallization from $95\%$ ethanol. To crystallize the dibenzalacetone, use $3 - 4\mathrm{mL}$ of $95\%$ ethanol for every gram of crude product. Place the crude product and ethanol in a large reaction vial. Seal with a Teflon cap and place in a hot water setup for a few minutes. Remove from the hot water bath and shake the mixture until the dibenzalacetone is completely dissolved in hot ethanol. Let the vial cool for a few minutes on the counter top, and then place it in an ice water bath. Bright yellow crystals will form. Isolate the product by filtration or by decanting the ethanol and placing the crystals on a piece of filter paper to dry. 

19. Weigh the recrystallized dibenzalacetone, calculate the percent yield. 

20. Determine the melting point of your product and compare your result with the literature value. 

21. The product can be further characterized by adding 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine to the ketone to form the ozazone. The 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine derivative of dibenzalacetone melts at $180^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ .
